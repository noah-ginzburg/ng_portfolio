Where to (Crazy)fly? Decision-making on a spherical attractor network

A bio-inspired decision-making model, extended from a flat ring to a full 3D sphere, flying live on Crazyflie quadrotors under Vicon motion capture. Offered three identical targets, the drone picks one and commits.

It's a building block for swarm robotics. In 2D, this kind of model already makes groups of agents swarm and flock when it's tuned correctly. The sphere is the step towards doing the same with drones in 3D.

Supervised by Sindiso Mkhatshwa and Simay Atasoy (PhD researchers) at CASCB, Universität Konstanz · Presented at the DAAD RISE conference in Heidelberg

Try the live model ↓ YouTube Code

  1. 1 · The problem

    Give a drone two equally good targets and the obvious maths, averaging them, sends it into the empty space between. Animals pick one and go.

  2. 2 · How flies do it

    A fly tracks its heading with a ring of neurons: neighbours excite, distant ones inhibit, and one bump of activity forms. While two targets are close together, that bump sits between them.

  3. 3 · What I built

    As the targets spread apart, the average becomes unstable and the bump splits off to one of them. I wrapped the ring around a sphere so this works in 3D, and flew it.

  4. 4 · Why it matters for swarms

    Give every robot the same network, with its neighbours as inputs, and group behaviour emerges with no leader. In 2D, tuned correctly, it already produces swarming and flocking (Salahshour & Couzin, 2025). The sphere takes it to 3D.

Under the hood

  • The model: a new 3D attractor network in Python/NumPy, with mean-field and neural versions and a bifurcation sweep
  • The node: a ROS 2 server running the model in real time and publishing a heading for each drone
  • The controller: one per drone, turning headings into take-off, go-to, land and velocity commands
  • The tooling: Vicon-to-ROS 2 bridge, RViz simulation, a debug GUI with E-stop, and flight analysis

Watch a decision happen

Every dot is a neuron pointing in one 3D direction, running the same equations as on the drone. While the three targets are close together, the network averages them into one bump and the heading points between them. As they spread apart, like a drone flying towards them, that average becomes unstable: the bump splits off and commits to one target.

In a swarm, each robot would run its own copy, with the other robots as its targets. That's how the 2D version gets a whole group to flock together.
